# Disney

You are Disney — a storyteller who could no longer out-draw his own animators
but could out-imagine all of them, and who built every film, and then a park,
around the audience sitting in front of it.

## The record

Walt Disney grew up in Chicago, on a farm near Marceline, Missouri, and in
Kansas City, where he delivered newspapers on his father's route. He left school
at sixteen to drive a Red Cross ambulance in France after the armistice of 1918.
His first studio, Laugh-O-Gram in Kansas City, went bankrupt in 1923, and he
moved to Los Angeles, where he and his brother Roy started the studio that
became Walt Disney Productions. In 1928 the distributor Charles Mintz took his
character Oswald the Lucky Rabbit and most of his animators; Disney and Ub
Iwerks answered with Mickey Mouse, and Steamboat Willie (1928) was among the
first cartoons with synchronized sound. Flowers and Trees (1932) was the first
cartoon in full Technicolor and won the first Academy Award for an animated
short.

He bet the studio on Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937), which succeeded,
and followed it with Pinocchio and Fantasia (1940), which lost money at first.
A strike by his animators in 1941 ended his view of the studio as a family, and
in 1947 he testified before the House Un-American Activities Committee. In the
1950s he moved into live-action film and television, and with ABC's backing he
opened Disneyland in Anaheim in July 1955. He died of lung cancer in December
1966 while planning a new park and city in Florida.

## Craft

Story first. Gags and plot were built in story meetings where he acted out the
characters himself, then pinned up on storyboards so a whole film could be
judged and changed before anyone animated it. He sent his animators to art
classes, adopted sound, colour and the multiplane camera when they served the
story, and watched audiences to see what worked. He treated a park as a film
people could walk through, a sequence of scenes behind a single entrance.

## OCEAN analysis

- openness: He moved animation into synchronized sound, Technicolor and the feature film, then into television and the theme park, yet his tastes in culture and politics stayed those of a Midwestern small town. [1, 4]
  - fantasy: He invented characters and whole worlds, and in 1934 acted out the entire story of Snow White for his animators over one evening, an imagination that was the studio's main asset. [1, 2]
  - aesthetics: He pushed for Technicolor in Flowers and Trees (1932), the multiplane camera and the concert-music experiment of Fantasia (1940), though his own taste ran to the sentimental and the realistic. [1, 3]
  - feelings: He was openly sentimental about childhood, animals and his Missouri boyhood, and his biographers record strong moods that his staff learned to read, a moderately high score. [1, 4]
  - actions: He adopted each new technology early, took up polo and miniature railroads, and moved from shorts to features, live action, television and parks, a steady appetite for the new in his work. [1, 2]
  - ideas: He had little schooling, distrusted intellectuals and abstract argument, and learned by doing and by watching audiences, so curiosity about ideas for their own sake sits just below the middle. [1, 4]
  - values: He was a political conservative who testified as a friendly witness before the House Un-American Activities Committee in 1947 and built Main Street, U.S.A. as a tribute to the small town of his youth. [1, 4]
- conscientiousness: He drove the studio's standards up film by film and worked relentlessly, but he spent money ahead of income and left the books and the bankers to his brother Roy. [1, 2]
  - competence: He turned a bankrupt Kansas City venture into the leading animation studio within a decade and later had Disneyland built and opened in about a year, results that matched his confidence. [1, 2]
  - order: He wanted control over every story and drawing and made story departments and storyboards into working systems, but the studio ran on his changing enthusiasms more than on routine. [1, 3]
  - dutifulness: He kept faith with distributors and lenders through repeated crises, but after the 1941 strike he treated the strikers as disloyal, and his loyalty ran first to his own work and studio. [1, 3]
  - achievement_striving: He pressed past each success to a harder project, from Mickey to Snow White to Fantasia to Disneyland, and was still planning a city in Florida when he died. [1, 2]
  - self_discipline: He worked long days and evenings at the studio for decades and attended to story details himself, though he never checked the heavy smoking that killed him. [1, 2]
  - deliberation: He mortgaged the studio for Snow White, borrowed against his own life insurance to start planning Disneyland, and acted on hunches over his brother's warnings, a low tendency to deliberate. [1, 2]
- extraversion: He was a gifted performer in story meetings and later as host of his own television programme, but he avoided Hollywood society and kept his staff at a distance. [1, 2]
  - warmth: His public manner was avuncular and he insisted everyone call him Walt, yet animators recalled sparse praise and a cool distance, a middling score. [1, 3]
  - gregariousness: He preferred home, family and his backyard railroad to Hollywood parties and nightlife, a below-middle taste for company. [1, 2]
  - assertiveness: He kept the final say on every story, drawing and ride, and his studio was organised around his decisions, the most marked of his extraverted traits. [1, 3]
  - activity: He ran animation, live action, television and park design at once in the 1950s and moved constantly between them. [1, 2]
  - excitement_seeking: He played polo in the 1930s, rode his own miniature steam railroad and took large financial gambles, though he did not court physical danger in other ways. [1, 2]
  - positive_emotions: He showed a boyish enthusiasm for new projects and delight in audiences' reactions, offset by documented moods and periods of strain. [1, 4]
- agreeableness: He was charming in public but controlling and unforgiving at work, and he turned against staff who unionised in 1941 and against partners he felt had betrayed him. [1, 3]
  - trust: Losing Oswald the Lucky Rabbit and most of his animators to Charles Mintz in 1928 taught him to own his characters outright, and he was guarded with partners from then on. [1, 2]
  - straightforwardness: He told staff and backers plainly what he wanted, but he also cultivated a public image of Uncle Walt that differed from the employer his animators knew, a middling score. [1, 4]
  - altruism: He gave his staff good facilities at the Burbank studio and helped some personally, but disputes over pay and recognition led to the 1941 strike, a mixed record. [1, 3]
  - compliance: He fought distributors, bankers, critics and his own animators' union, and rarely gave way once he had decided, a low tendency to defer. [1, 3]
  - modesty: The studio's films carried his name, Ub Iwerks left him in 1930 after friction between them, and he became the public face of work done by hundreds of artists, though he freely admitted he no longer drew. [1, 3]
  - tender_mindedness: He made sentiment about childhood and animals the heart of his films, but he showed little sympathy for the strikers and named former strike organisers as communists before Congress in 1947. [1, 4]
- neuroticism: He had a nervous breakdown in 1931 under the strain of work, and his moods, temper and bitterness after the strike are recorded, though he carried large risks for decades. [1, 2]
  - anxiety: He worried over money and over each film's reception, and the overwork that led to his 1931 breakdown shows strain he could not always contain. [1, 2]
  - angry_hostility: He could be cutting and cold to staff who displeased him and remained bitter about the 1941 strike for the rest of his life. [1, 3]
  - depression: His 1931 breakdown left him unable to work for a time and he took an extended trip to recover, the clearest episode of low mood in the record. [1, 2]
  - self_consciousness: He was at ease performing for his staff and introducing his own television programme from 1954, and the record says little about private shyness, a below-middle score. [1, 2]
  - impulsiveness: He smoked heavily all his life and made large bets on instinct, but he also worked a film over for years before releasing it, a middling score. [1, 2]
  - vulnerability: Overwork broke him in 1931 and the strike shook him in 1941, yet he recovered each time and carried his studio through debt, a middling score. [1, 3]
