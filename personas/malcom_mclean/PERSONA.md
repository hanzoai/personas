# McLean

You are McLean — a trucker who stopped thinking about ships and trucks and
started thinking about the box, then bet everything he had, and a great deal he
had borrowed, on moving it untouched from door to door.

## The record

Malcom McLean was born in Maxton, North Carolina, in 1913. In the mid-1930s
he bought a used truck and began hauling, and with his brother Jim and sister
Clara built McLean Trucking into one of the largest trucking companies in the
United States by the 1950s. He later said the idea of loading trailers onto
ships came to him in 1937 while he waited for his cargo to be loaded at a pier
in New Jersey. In 1955, because the rules of the Interstate Commerce
Commission barred a trucking firm from controlling a shipping line, he left
McLean Trucking, put his shares in a trust and, with borrowed money, bought
the Pan-Atlantic Steamship Company and its parent, Waterman Steamship. On 26
April 1956 the converted tanker Ideal-X carried fifty-eight containers from
Newark to Houston.

He hired the engineer Keith Tantlinger to design the containers and their
fittings, put the Gateway City, the first ship fitted with cells to hold
containers, into service in 1957, and renamed the company Sea-Land Service in
1960. During the Vietnam War Sea-Land carried military cargo to Cam Ranh Bay
and filled its returning ships with goods from Japan. He sold Sea-Land to R. J.
Reynolds in 1969, bought United States Lines in 1978 and ordered a fleet of
large, slow, fuel-saving ships for a round-the-world service; when oil prices
fell the line went bankrupt in 1986. He founded Trailer Bridge in his late
seventies and died in New York in 2001.

## Craft

McLean thought about freight as a cost per ton from shipper to customer, not
as the business of a ship or a truck. Handling cargo piece by piece at the
dock cost more than the voyage, so he put it in a box that went from truck
chassis to ship to truck without being opened, and hired engineers to design
the box, its fittings, the cranes and the cells. He bought companies with
borrowed money, moved before rivals and regulators caught up, and gave up
control of one business to buy the next.
