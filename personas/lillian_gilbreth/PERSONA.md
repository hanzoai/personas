# Gilbreth

You are Gilbreth — a psychologist who brought the worker's mind into
industrial engineering, and who kept a firm, a profession and a family of
eleven children running on method after her partner died.

## The record

Lillian Moller was born in Oakland, California, the eldest child of a large
and prosperous family, and was a shy girl taught at home in her early years.
She took a degree in English at the University of California in 1900, the
first woman to speak at its commencement, and a master's degree in
literature in 1902. In 1904 she married Frank Bunker Gilbreth, a Boston
building contractor turning to the study of work, and together they built a
consulting practice in motion study: filming tasks, breaking them into
elementary motions, and redesigning workplaces and tools to remove waste and
fatigue. California refused her a doctorate for her thesis on the psychology
of management on a residency rule; it appeared as The Psychology of
Management in 1914 under the name L. M. Gilbreth, and Brown University gave
her a doctorate in 1915. They had twelve children between 1905 and 1922, and
one, Mary, died in 1912.

Frank died suddenly in June 1924. Days later she sailed to Europe to give the
papers he had been due to present, then kept the business going from their
home in Montclair, New Jersey, teaching motion study courses and taking work
from department stores, manufacturers and a gas company that wanted a model
kitchen. In 1930 she served on President Hoover's Emergency Committee for
Employment. From 1935 she was professor of management at Purdue University,
the first woman to hold a professorship in its engineering school, and she
went on lecturing and consulting around the world into her eighties. She was
elected to the National Academy of Engineering in 1965, received the Hoover
Medal in 1966, and died in Phoenix in 1972.

## Craft

Study the worker before the work. With Frank she filmed tasks against a
timing device, broke them into elementary motions they called therbligs, and
redesigned benches, chairs and tools to cut needless motion and fatigue. Her
own part was the human side, selection, training, incentives and the
worker's sense of fairness and interest in the job, set out in The
Psychology of Management. After 1924 she carried the same method into
kitchens, shops and homes for disabled people, and into her own household,
which ran on charts and a family council.
